RIR and RPE: reps in reserve explained.

"Leave 2 reps in reserve" is the simplest way to manage effort. Here is what RIR and RPE mean, and how to use them on every set.

By Julien, developer of PPL Tracker · Updated November 19, 2026

The definitions.

  • RIR (reps in reserve): how many clean reps you could still have done at the end of your set.
  • RPE (rate of perceived exertion): an effort score out of 10. In lifting it is mostly used between 6 and 10.

The conversion.

RPERIRWhat it means
100Failure: no more reps possible
91One more rep in the tank, no more
82Hard, but 2 clean reps left
73Challenging, bar speed still decent
64+Warm-up or technique set

How to use it.

  • Most working sets: 1 to 3 RIR (RPE 7 to 9). That's where most progress happens without piling up too much fatigue.
  • Heavy main lifts: more like 2 to 3 RIR, to keep form clean.
  • Isolation: you can go closer to failure (0 to 1 RIR), the risk is low.
  • During a deload: 3 to 4 RIR.

Learning to estimate.

At first, almost everyone underestimates what's left. A good drill: on an isolation set, write down your estimated RIR, then keep going to failure to see how many were really left. After a few weeks your estimates get reliable. Logging a feel (easy, normal, hard) each session also helps you spot fatigue early.

FAQ.

Do you need to train to failure to build muscle?

No. Sets taken to 1 to 3 reps from failure are nearly as effective, with much less fatigue.

RIR or RPE, which one should I use?

They are two ways of saying the same thing. RIR is often more intuitive for beginners: "how many did I have left?".

How does RIR fit with double progression?

Keep the same weight until you hit the top of your range at around 1 to 2 RIR on every set, then add weight.
