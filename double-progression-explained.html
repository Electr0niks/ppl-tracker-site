Double progression, explained simply.

"When should I add weight?" Double progression answers that with zero math: reps first, then weight.

By Julien, developer of PPL Tracker · Updated November 19, 2026

The principle.

Every exercise has a rep range, for example 3 sets of 8 to 12. Keep the same weight and add reps from session to session. Once you hit the top of the range on every set, add weight and start again at the bottom of the range.

Two variables progress one after the other (reps, then weight): hence the name.

A concrete example.

Bench press, target 3 × 8–12:

SessionWeightSet 1Set 2Set 3
160 kg1098
260 kg11109
360 kg121110
460 kg121212
562.5 kg988

In session 4 every set hits 12, so add 2.5 kg (5 lb). In session 5 the reps drop back down. That's expected, and the cycle starts again.

The rules that make it work.

  • Sensible ranges: 5–8 or 6–10 on big lifts, 8–12 or 10–15 on isolation.
  • Small jumps: +2.5 kg (5 lb) upper body, +5 kg (10 lb) legs; on isolation, the smallest jump available.
  • Same technique: a rep gained by cheating doesn't count.
  • Keep 1 to 3 reps in reserve on most sets.
  • Log everything: without your last session in front of you, you can't know whether to aim for one more rep or more weight.

And when it stalls?

If you stall for two or three sessions on several lifts, that's not failure: it's often a sign you need a deload week, or that sleep and food aren't keeping up.

FAQ.

Does double progression work for beginners?

Yes, it is the ideal method to start with: simple, no percentages, and it stops you from adding weight too early.

Do I need to hit the top of the range on every set?

That is the safest rule. Some people add weight once the first set hits the top; it is faster but you stall more often.

What if I can't add 2.5 kg?

With dumbbells or machines with big jumps, widen the range (for example 10–15) or add a set before increasing the weight.
